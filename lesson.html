Designing AI-Resilient Assignments

Frameworks, scales, and practical strategies to preserve assessment validity in the age of generative AI. Move from detection to intentional design.

Why This Workshop Matters

92% of students now use AI in their studies (HEPI, 2025). 88% acknowledge using generative AI for assessed work. Yet AI detection tools show false positive rates up to 61.3% for non-native English speakers — making surveillance-based approaches both unreliable and inequitable.

The solution is not better detection — it is better design. This workshop equips faculty with validated frameworks and practical strategies to create assignments that remain meaningful measures of learning when AI access is assumed.

Theoretical Foundations

This workshop is grounded in six established learning theories. These are not decorative citations — each one directly shapes a design decision in the strategies you will learn today.

Backward Design

Wiggins & McTighe (2005)

Start with desired outcomes, then define acceptable evidence, then plan learning activities. Every redesigned assignment in this workshop follows this sequence — outcomes first, AIAS level second, strategies third.

Constructive Alignment

Biggs & Tang (2011)

Learning outcomes, teaching activities, and assessment tasks must be aligned to the same intended learning. The AIAS framework is fundamentally a constructive alignment tool — it asks: does the AI use level match the outcome you are assessing?

Bloom's Revised Taxonomy

Anderson & Krathwohl (2001)

Cognitive levels from Remember to Create. AI handles lower-order tasks competently; higher-order tasks — evaluate, create with original judgment — remain distinctively human. This is your diagnostic tool for AI vulnerability.

Andragogy

Knowles (1980)

Adult learners need relevance, autonomy, and immediate application. This workshop is designed on those principles: you work with your own assignments, choose your own AIAS levels, and leave with a redesigned artifact ready for your next semester.

Cognitive Load Theory

Sweller (1988)

Working memory is limited. Rather than asking you to juggle all seven strategies at once, the Resilience Stack gives you a three-layer heuristic: process, personalization, verification. Layer two or three — not all seven.

TPACK

Mishra & Koehler (2006)

Effective teaching with technology requires integrating technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge. Today you are building TPACK for the AI era — understanding what AI can do (technology), how to design assessments (pedagogy), and what matters in your discipline (content).

The Evidence: Why Redesign, Not Detect

Three converging pillars of evidence make the case for assignment redesign over detection-based approaches.

Student AI Use Is Near-Universal

92% use AI in studies — any use (HEPI, 2025)
88% use GenAI for graded submissions (up from 53%)
5% of students know their institution's AI policy

AI use among students is normative, not exceptional.

Detection Tools Are Unreliable

61% false positive rate for non-native English speakers
28% of international STEM students received lower grades from false flags

Detection transfers the integrity burden to an algorithm that disproportionately harms our most vulnerable students. — Giray, Roe, & Diesta Espiritu (2026)

Faculty Consensus Supports Redesign

92% of faculty concerned about AI-facilitated dishonesty
84% agree AI reduces critical thinking and originality

Universal use + unreliable detection + faculty concern = assignment redesign is the most effective response.

The Open-Book Exam Parallel

"When calculators entered mathematics classrooms, we did not ban calculators — we redesigned what we asked students to do with them. When open-book exams emerged, we did not eliminate exams — we wrote questions that required application, not recall. Generative AI is the next iteration of that same pedagogical challenge. The question is not how to prevent access, but how to design assessments that remain meaningful when access is assumed."

Quick Diagnostic for Your Assignment

Look at your assignment prompt. What verb does it use? If the primary verb lives at Remember or Understand (define, list, summarize, explain), AI can likely complete it at a passing level. If it lives at Evaluate or Create (judge with personal criteria, design from lived experience, synthesize original insight), AI struggles without authentic human input. Your redesign goal: push at least one element of every major assignment into Evaluate or Create territory — or anchor lower-order tasks in situated, personal, or process-based contexts that AI cannot replicate.

The AI Assessment Scale (AIAS)

Developed by Perkins, Furze, Roe, & MacVaugh (2024). Adopted by hundreds of institutions worldwide. Translated into 30+ languages. Replaces binary "AI allowed / AI banned" policies with a graduated, assignment-level approach.

Key AIAS Design Principles

No Level Is Inherently Better

The appropriate level depends on the learning outcome, not a moral judgment about AI use. Version 2 abandoned the red-to-green color scheme to avoid implying a value hierarchy.

Assignment-Level, Not Course-Level

A single course might include Level 1 assessments (proctored midterm), Level 2 tasks (research proposal), and Level 4 activities (AI-assisted data analysis).

Validity at All Levels

"Assessment security, in the traditional sense, is only possible at Level 1, but validity — whether the assessment measures what it claims — is achievable throughout." — Phillip Dawson

Transparency Is the Mechanism

Students know the AI use level for each assignment in advance. This replaces ambiguous syllabus policies with clear, assignment-specific expectations.

Analogy — Lab Safety Levels

"Think of the AIAS like laboratory safety levels. A BSL-1 lab handles low-risk agents with standard practices. A BSL-4 lab handles dangerous pathogens with maximum containment. Neither level is 'better' — each is appropriate to its purpose. Similarly, a Level 1 assessment (no AI) is appropriate when you need to verify foundational knowledge, while a Level 4 assessment (full AI) is appropriate when AI fluency is the learning outcome."

Key Takeaways

1

The question is no longer whether students will use AI, but whether our assignments remain valid measures of learning when they do.

2

AI resilience is not about prohibition — it is about intentional design. The AIAS gives us a shared vocabulary for making AI use decisions transparent and pedagogically grounded, assignment by assignment.

3

The most resilient assignments share a common architecture: they are process-oriented, personally situated, and verified through human interaction. Layered strategies create meaningful resistance.
